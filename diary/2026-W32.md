## What worked / what didn't:
**Discretionary:**
- Trimming from 10 to 4 shares on 08-03 was the right process even though price recovered afterward, the support/channel break was real and visible at the time of the decision, and judging the trim by whether it made money in hindsight would be the wrong standard. Process quality and outcome are separate questions, and by the process standard, this holds up.
- Keeping 4 shares rather than exiting fully meant the position still benefited from the recovery while genuinely reducing risk during the most uncertain days, a middle-ground decision that reflected real uncertainty rather than forcing false confidence in either direction.
- More general principle worth keeping: evaluate a decision by whether the reasoning was sound given the information available at the time, not by how it happened to turn out. Worth having this as a standing standard for every future trade review, not just this one.

**Systematic:**
- The ATR-based volatility layer performed exactly as designed: it stayed silent during a noisier, non-extreme trend wobble (07-22 to 07-24) and only flagged the one day that was genuinely unusual (07-31), suggesting the threshold is well-calibrated for a first attempt, not just noisy or oversensitive.
- Confirmed directly that 10/20 still hadn't reacted four days after the shock, while 3/7 caught it same-day, reinforcing last week's finding rather than a one-off.
- Built something qualitatively different from last week: not just another signal, but an actual decision-making system, a three-way output (hold/partial sell/full sell) combining trend, volatility, and real P&L, rather than a single boolean flag.
- The three-zone sizing rule (below 10% of holding = full sell, above 90% = hold, in between = partial sell) was my own design, worked out through reasoning about a concrete example (would you sell 1 share out of 200 just because the model suggested 199), not copied from anywhere.
- The combined decide_action() logic worked correctly on its first real run against live data: position is at a loss, so the hard loss-protection rule correctly blocked any sell regardless of what the trend or volatility signals said, exactly the intended behaviour.
- Didn't fix the single-symbol limitation (strategy.py hardcoded to AAPL) despite knowing about it, a deliberate scope decision given the size of the actual fix required.

## Comparison (systematic vs discretionary):
**Similarities:**
- Both tracks remain in the same underlying position (AAPL) and are both still net negative overall despite the week's recovery, discretionary ~-8% (4 shares remaining), systematic ~-8.5% (5 shares, untouched). Neither track has actually closed out the position yet, both are still holding through an unresolved question: is this a genuine recovery or a temporary bounce before further downside.

**Differences in process:**
- Discretionary took real action this week (the trim on 08-03), based on a specific, visible piece of evidence, a broken support/channel structure. Systematic took no action on the actual position, its decide_action() output was "hold" throughout, blocked by a hard rule that refuses to sell at any loss regardless of what the trend or volatility signals say.
- This exposes a genuine philosophical difference, not just a technical one. The systematic rule treats "loss" as the deciding factor, full stop, whereas the discretionary decision treated the loss as incidental, what actually mattered was what the broken support structure implied about the trend, the loss was just a byproduct of that same price move.
- Systematic's real output this week was infrastructure, not action, a genuinely more sophisticated decision engine now exists (three-way hold/partial/full sell logic, volatility-aware sizing), but it's never actually been triggered into doing anything other than hold, since the position has stayed underwater the whole time.

**Open question:**
- The systematic side's absolute loss-protection rule avoided cutting losses this week, which happened to work out since AAPL recovered. But that's not the same as the rule being "correct," it just hasn't yet been tested in a scenario where the price kept falling after a real structural break, which is exactly what discretionary's reasoning was built to catch. Short-term, discretionary's willingness to act on a loss looks more responsive; but if AAPL's long-term trend genuinely holds and this was just a normal pullback, systematic's refusal to sell may prove to be the better long-term call, purely by doing nothing. The real test isn't this week, it's the next time there's a real, sustained breakdown rather than a recoverable dip, that's when it'll become clear whether "never sell at a loss" is a discipline or a blind spot.
